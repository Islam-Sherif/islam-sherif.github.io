Experience.

Companies are built twice — once as a strategy, and once as an organisation that can carry it. I work on the second build, and in my experience it decides the first.

I am Director of People, Culture & Corporate Affairs at The Enterprise Company — seven publications read by MENA's business and policy leadership, an advisory practice, an events business, and some 250 people. I came to the role through investor relations, business analysis, project management and corporate affairs: cross-functional range built one function at a time rather than claimed all at once. What follows is the set of problems I have spent my career inside, written down in the hope that it is useful to someone standing where I have stood.

01

Strategy execution.

The gap between a strategy and its delivery is almost never ambition or talent. It is that the strategy was never decomposed — and a paragraph that reads “expand into adjacent verticals” is a heading, not a plan.

Someone has to break the intent into work, sequence that work against real dependencies, put a single name against each branch, and set the cadence at which those names answer for it. The failure I have watched most often is quieter than missed deadlines: strategy is announced at an all-hands and then never appears again in the operating rhythm, living in the deck and nowhere in the calendar. The second failure is the opposite — the plan exists and is quietly wrong, because the operating environment moved. In Egypt it moves. Re-planning has to be a routine operation rather than a crisis, or a company ends up defending a plan it stopped believing in months earlier.

  • Work breakdown and sequencing
  • Single-owner accountability
  • Decision cadence and operating rhythm
  • Re-planning under volatility
02

Organisational design.

An org chart is a capital allocation decision. Every role is a recurring cost carrying an expected return, and most companies never write down what that return was supposed to be.

I came to this from credit. A year underwriting corporate balance sheets teaches you to read an organisation the way a bank reads a borrower — trajectory before symptom, and never one number in isolation. Applied to headcount it means asking what the business case requires before asking who feels overloaded, because overload is a symptom and very often a symptom of poor sequencing rather than missing people. The uncomfortable half of the work is subtraction. Sometimes the right answer is that a layer should come out, or that a function should not exist yet, and companies find it easy to justify a hire and nearly impossible to justify a removal — which is how a structure that nobody designed gets inherited by everybody.

  • Structure against the business case
  • Workforce planning and role design
  • Attrition read as an asset-quality signal
  • Where a layer should be removed
03

The second manager layer.

Nearly every scaling company hits the same wall in the same place, and it is rarely the wall they were watching for.

Up to the first layer of management the founder or managing director is still the operating system: decisions route through one person, and it works. Add a second layer and that stops working almost overnight. The people now managing managers were promoted for individual output, the decision rights were never written down, and the company slows at precisely the moment it believes it is scaling. The fix is structural: what decisions belong at each layer, what escalates and to whom, what a manager is genuinely accountable for, and how performance is calibrated across departments so that a promotion means the same thing in two different rooms. Written down, the layer holds. Left implicit, the layer is re-hired in eighteen months by a leadership team convinced it picked the wrong people.

It is a design problem, sold as a training problem.

04

Governance and decision rights.

Ask a leadership team where a decision was made and you will often get a story about a corridor conversation. That is workable at twenty people. At two hundred it means nobody can reconstruct why anything was decided.

The same argument is then re-litigated every quarter by people who were not in the corridor. Governance is an unfairly boring word for the fix, because the fix is mostly about speed — decision rights, escalation paths, and a forum where cross-functional calls are made in front of the people who have to live with them. I convene executive sessions for that reason: not to inform people, but to move ownership into a room where it can be witnessed. The same framework carries crisis. A protocol written under pressure is worthless; written in advance, naming who speaks, who decides and what the thresholds are, it is the difference between a difficult week and a reputational event.

  • Decision rights and escalation paths
  • Executive convening and forum design
  • Crisis protocol and thresholds
  • Risk framework and institutional memory
05

Institutional and regulatory affairs.

Government and regulatory relationships are usually treated as networking: someone senior knows someone, and that is the strategy. It works until that person is unavailable, or leaves.

Treated as an operating discipline it looks different — mapped counterparts, a system for follow-through, and institutional memory that survives turnover on either side of the table. The objective is that a company is continuously legible to the institutions that regulate it, rather than urgently legible whenever there is a problem. In this region that is not a compliance nicety but an operating input, and the companies that build it early spend far less on it later, in money and in the kind of delay that never appears on an invoice.

  • Counterpart mapping and coverage
  • Licensing, policy and compliance engagement
  • Follow-through systems
  • Institutional memory beyond individuals

The second build decides the first.
