Experience.

Five functions, one at a time. Which is why strategy, structure, compliance and governance look like one problem to me rather than four departments arguing.

Someone who arrives at organisational design from human resources sees culture and capability. Someone who arrives from finance sees cost. Coming through all of it, an org chart looks like what it actually is — a multi-year capital commitment to a set of bets about where value gets created.

How I work.

One senior operator, not a team of juniors.

When a large firm takes on organisational work, the partner appears at the start and at the review; the work itself is done by people a decade younger than the managers they are advising, using a template built for a different market. I do the work myself. That caps how much I can carry at once, and it is the point rather than a limitation.

The output is documents you operate from.

A decision-rights map, a role charter, a hiring sequence with a cost model, a compliance calendar with a name against each obligation, an escalation path that resolves. If the end of the work is a presentation summarising your own company back to you, nothing was built.

Sometimes the answer is no.

Some problems are not structural. Some are not urgent. Some are cheaper to live with than to fix. Saying so is part of the work — anyone who has never talked someone out of a piece of work is not giving advice.

Built for a market that moves.

Egypt moves: currency, cost base, regulation, hiring markets. A plan built at one policy rate is not the same plan at another with different numbers in it — pricing changes, payment terms change, what is worth financing changes. Re-planning has to be routine here rather than a crisis, and frameworks imported from stable markets quietly assume it never happens.
